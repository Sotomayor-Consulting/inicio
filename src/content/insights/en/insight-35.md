The **digital nomad** lifestyle — working remotely while traveling the world — has grown enormously. However, many digital nomads are unaware of their tax obligations in the United States. Understanding **taxes for digital nomads** is key to avoiding penalties and taking advantage of available tax benefits.

Below, we explain everything you need to know about your tax situation as a digital nomad.

## 1. Are You a U.S. Tax Resident?

Your tax obligation depends on your **tax residency**. The IRS determines tax residency through two tests:

### Green Card Test:
If you are a lawful permanent resident, you are a tax resident and are taxed on your worldwide income.

### Substantial Presence Test:
You must have been physically present in the U.S. for at least:
- **31 days** during the current year, and
- **183 days** total over the last 3 years (counting: all days of the current year + 1/3 of the previous year + 1/6 of the second preceding year)

If you meet this test, you are a tax resident and must report your global income.

## 2. Digital Nomad as a Nonresident

If you **do not** meet the substantial presence test, you are considered a **nonresident** for tax purposes. As a nonresident:
- You are only taxed on your **U.S.-source income**
- Income earned while working from abroad is generally **not subject to U.S. tax**
- You may qualify for tax treaty benefits

## 3. Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (FEIE)

One of the most powerful benefits for digital nomads is the **Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (FEIE)** . If you are a U.S. tax resident but live and work abroad, you can exclude a significant amount of your earned income from U.S. taxation (adjusted annually for inflation).

### Requirements:
- **Bona Fide Residence Test**: Be a resident of a foreign country for a full tax year without interruption
- **Physical Presence Test**: Be physically outside the U.S. for at least **330 full days** in any 12 consecutive months

If you qualify, you can exclude your foreign earned income up to the annual limit. You can also elect to exclude or claim a credit for foreign housing expenses (Foreign Housing Exclusion or Deduction).

## 4. Where Do You Pay Taxes? The Key: Where Work Is Performed

For a digital nomad, the physical location where you work determines taxation:

- **Working from the U.S.** → U.S.-source income → taxed in the U.S.
- **Working from abroad** → Foreign-source income → possible FEIE exclusion
- **Working for a foreign client** → Generally foreign-source income
- **Working for a U.S. client** → Source depends on where you physically perform the work

> **Important:** Where your client is located **does not** determine the source of income; what matters is **where you perform the work**.

## 5. Self-Employment and SE Tax as a Digital Nomad

If you work as an independent contractor (freelancer), you must pay **Self-Employment Tax (SE Tax)** of 15.3%. However:

- The **FEIE only excludes foreign earned income from Income Tax**, not from SE Tax
- This means even if you qualify for FEIE, you may still owe SE Tax on your service income
- Exception: If your income is covered by another country's social security system (under a **Totalization Agreement** between the U.S. and that country), you may be exempt from SE Tax

## 6. Foreign Bank Account Reporting (FBAR)

As a digital nomad, you likely have foreign bank accounts. If the aggregate value of all your foreign financial accounts exceeds **$10,000** at any time during the year, you must file the **FBAR (FinCEN Form 114)** .

### Consequences of not filing:
- Civil penalties of up to $10,000 per non-willful violation
- Penalties of up to $100,000 or 50% of the account balance (whichever is greater) for willful violations
- Potential criminal charges

## 7. FATCA and Form 8938

In addition to FBAR, if your foreign financial assets exceed certain thresholds, you must file **Form 8938** (Statement of Specified Foreign Financial Assets) with your tax return.

### Thresholds for residents living abroad:
- **$200,000** if filing jointly (Married Filing Jointly) and living abroad
- **$400,000** if asset value is higher (single or separate filers)

## 8. State Taxes and Residency

While the IRS is federal, states may also claim taxes. As a digital nomad:

- If you establish **residency in a state with no income tax** (like Texas, Florida, Nevada), you avoid state income tax
- If you move abroad, your last state of residence may still claim taxes if you maintain connections (driver's license, voter registration, bank address)
- To break state residency, you must sever significant ties with that state

## 9. Social Security Totalization Agreements

The U.S. has agreements with several countries to avoid double social security taxation. If you work as a digital nomad in a country with a Totalization Agreement:

- You only pay social security in **one country**
- The agreement determines which country has priority based on the time you work there
- You may become exempt from SE Tax if you qualify under the agreement

## 10. Recommendations for Digital Nomads

### Best tax practices:
- **Keep a travel log**: Where you were each day and what work you performed
- **Maintain separate bank accounts**: One for the U.S. and another for abroad
- **Get international health insurance**: While the individual mandate penalty was eliminated, coverage is still prudent
- **File your taxes on time**: Even if you owe no tax, failure to file can result in penalties
- **Consult a specialist**: Tax laws for digital nomads are complex and frequently change

## Conclusion

**Taxes for digital nomads** involve multiple factors: tax residency, source of income, treaties, FEIE, FBAR, and more. The key is understanding your specific situation and planning carefully to maximize benefits and minimize risks.
