**Receiving a letter from the IRS is one of the most stressful events for any business owner or individual taxpayer — but most IRS letters are routine and easy to resolve.** The IRS sends over 200 million notices and letters each year, and the vast majority are simple requests for information, payment reminders, or correction notices.

The key is knowing how to read the notice, understanding what it requires, and responding within the deadline. Ignoring an IRS letter is the only truly bad move — it escalates quickly from a simple notice to liens, levies, and enforcement actions.

In this guide, we cover **what to do if the IRS sent you a letter in 2026**: how to identify the type of notice, IRS notice numbers explained, response timeline and urgency by notice type, how to respond step-by-step, common notices for business owners, what to do if you cannot pay, how to get professional help, and how to prevent future notices.

## 1. How to Read an IRS Notice

### Anatomy of an IRS Letter

Every IRS notice follows a standard format. Understanding the layout helps you quickly determine what is needed.

```
ANATOMY OF AN IRS NOTICE:

  HEADER:
    Department of the Treasury — Internal Revenue Service
    [Address of IRS Service Center]
    
  NOTICE IDENTIFICATION:
    Notice/Letter Number: CP##### or LTR ####
    Taxpayer Identification Number: XXX-XX-####
    Notice Date: [Date]
    Tax Period/Year: [Tax Year]
    Response Due Date: [Date]

  BODY:
    What the IRS identified as incorrect or outstanding
    Amount due or refund adjustment
    Explanation of the issue
    Legal authority for the notice

  ACTION REQUIRED:
    What you need to do
    Deadline for response
    Consequences if no response

  CONTACT INFORMATION:
    Phone number to call
    Address to write to
    Website reference (IRS.gov)

  TAXPAYER RIGHTS:
    Taxpayer Bill of Rights summary
    Appeal rights
    Representation information
    Payment plan options
```

## 2. First Steps When You Receive an IRS Letter

### Immediate Action Checklist

```
IRS LETTER — IMMEDIATE ACTION CHECKLIST:

  DAY 1:
    ✓ Open the letter immediately (do not set it aside)
    ✓ Read the entire notice carefully
    ✓ Note the notice number (CP#### or LTR ####)
    ✓ Note the response due date
    ✓ Determine the type of action required

  DAY 2-3:
    ✓ Verify the information against your records
    ✓ Check if you already responded or resolved this
    ✓ Gather any supporting documentation
    ✓ Identify if the notice came from a scam (see below)
    ✓ Contact a tax professional if the amount is significant

  DAY 4-7:
    ✓ Draft your response (if required)
    ✓ Complete any requested forms or schedules
    ✓ Make payment arrangements (if balance due)
    ✓ Send response via certified mail
    ✓ Make a copy of everything for your records

  BEFORE DEADLINE:
    ✓ Confirm the IRS received your response
    ✓ Document your compliance
    ✓ Set a follow-up reminder (30-60 days)
    ✓ Save the notice and response in your permanent records
```

### How to Verify a Legitimate IRS Letter

```
SCAM PREVENTION — VERIFYING IRS LETTERS:

  LEGITIMATE IRS LETTER:
    • Has a notice number (CP#### or LTR ####)
    • Comes via USPS (IRS never uses email or text)
    • Provides specific tax information
    • Lists your taxpayer rights
    • Gives a specific phone number for your case
    • Can be verified on IRS.gov

  IRS WILL NEVER:
    • Call demanding immediate payment
    • Email or text with a link to sign in
    • Threaten police arrest or deportation
    • Demand payment via gift cards or wire transfer
    • Ask for credit card numbers over the phone
    • Send a link to verify personal information
    • Leave threatening voicemails

  HOW TO VERIFY:
    1. Go to IRS.gov and search the notice number
    2. Create or log in to your IRS online account
    3. Check your account transcripts for matching info
    4. Call the IRS at 1-800-829-1040 (not the number in the letter)
    5. Contact your tax preparer or CPA
    6. Use the IRS Identity Protection PIN tool
```

## 3. Responding to Common IRS Notices

### Balance Due Notices (CP14, CP161, CP22)

```
BALANCE DUE RESPONSE STRATEGY:

  STEP 1: VERIFY THE AMOUNT
    • Compare the notice to your tax return
    • Check for math errors on the IRS's part
    • Verify all credits and payments were applied
    • Review your IRS account transcript online

  STEP 2: DECIDE YOUR RESPONSE
    AGREE WITH THE AMOUNT:
      • Pay online at IRS.gov/pay (fastest)
      • Set up a payment plan (IRS.gov/opa)
      • Request penalty abatement if applicable
      • Pay by check with the response stub

    DISAGREE WITH THE AMOUNT:
      • Write a letter explaining why
      • Include supporting documentation
      • Reference the notice number and tax year
      • Send to the address on the notice
      • Request a hold on collection while under review

  STEP 3: PAYMENT OPTIONS
    Pay in Full: Best — stops penalties and interest
    Short-Term Extension (120 days): No setup fee, but interest accrues
    Installment Agreement: $31-$130 setup fee, monthly payments
    Partial Payment Installment: Based on ability to pay
    Offer in Compromise: Settle for less (complex, low approval rate)
    Currently Not Collectible: For financial hardship

  STEP 4: PREVENT FUTURE BALANCE DUE NOTICES
    • Adjust your withholding (W-4 form)
    • Make estimated quarterly payments
    • Schedule automatic reminders for filing
    • Work with a CPA for tax planning
```

### Math Error Notices (CP10, CP11, CP12)

```
MATH ERROR RESPONSE:

  COMMON MATH ERRORS:
    • Addition/subtraction mistakes
    • Incorrect tax table lookup
    • Standard deduction miscalculation
    • Dependent claimed on another return
    • Social Security number mismatch
    • Filing status inconsistency
    • Earned Income Tax Credit calculation

  IF YOU AGREE:
    • No response needed (IRS will adjust automatically)
    • Pay any additional amount shown on the notice
    • Receive refund if the notice shows a credit
    • Verify your corrected return online

  IF YOU DISAGREE:
    • Respond within 60 days of the notice date
    • Write "I disagree with the proposed adjustment"
    • Explain why the IRS is incorrect
    • Attach copies of supporting documents
    • Send to the address on the notice
    • Request a full examination if needed

  TIMELINE:
    Day 1: Receive math error notice
    Day 1-30: Review and decide response
    Day 31-60: If disagreeing, gather documentation
    Day 61: File response (must be within 60 days)
    Day 90-120: IRS processes response
    Day 120+: Notice becomes final if no response
```

### Underreported Income Notices (CP2000, CP2050)

```
CP2000 RESPONSE GUIDE:

  WHAT THE CP2000 MEANS:
    • IRS computers matched W-2s/1099s to your return
    • Found income reported to IRS not on your return
    • Proposed additional tax, penalties, and interest
    • This is NOT an audit — it is an underreporter inquiry

  EXAMPLE CP2000 SCENARIO:
    Your 2024 return reported $85,000 income
    IRS records show $92,000 (1099-NEC from freelance work)
    Proposed change: $7,000 additional income
    Additional tax: $1,540
    Penalties: $308
    Interest: $175
    Total due: $2,023

  RESPONSE OPTIONS:

    OPTION A: AGREE (IRS is correct)
      • Sign the response form (Form 4549)
      • Pay the amount due or set up a payment plan
      • Request penalty abatement if first-time
      • Case closed

    OPTION B: DISAGREE (IRS is wrong)
      • Check IRS records against your income documents
      • Identify the specific item the IRS has wrong
      • Write a detailed response letter
      • Attach evidence (1099 corrections, bank statements)
      • Request abatement of incorrect assessment

    OPTION C: PARTIAL AGREEMENT
      • Identify which items you agree with
      • Dispute specific items with documentation
      • Pay the undisputed amount
      • Request hold on disputed amount

  DEADLINES:
    CP2000 response: 30 days from notice date
    Disagreement: 60 days from notice date
    Tax Court petition: 90 days from assessment
```

### Audit Notices (LTR 105C, CP21, CP80)

```
AUDIT RESPONSE STRATEGY:

  TYPES OF AUDITS:
    Correspondence Audit — handled by mail (simplest)
    Office Audit — at an IRS office (moderate)
    Field Audit — at your home or business (most complex)
    Taxpayer Compliance Measurement Program — random selection

  WHAT THE IRS MAY REQUEST:
    • Income documentation (bank statements, invoices)
    • Expense receipts and supporting records
    • Mileage logs and travel documentation
    • Home office deduction justification
    • Business expense categorization
    • Charitable contribution receipts
    • Medical expense documentation
    • Education expense records

  AUDIT RESPONSE TIMELINE:

    Week 1: Read the audit notice carefully
    Week 2: Gather requested documents
    Week 3: Review documents with a tax professional
    Week 4: Submit response or request extension
    Week 5-8: IRS reviews documentation
    Week 9-12: IRS sends examination report
    Week 13: Agree, disagree, or request appeals

  IF YOU DISAGREE WITH AUDIT RESULTS:
    • Request a meeting with the auditor's manager
    • File a formal protest (Form 12203)
    • Request Appeals Conference
    • Petition Tax Court (within 90 days)
    • Consider mediation or arbitration

  COMMON AUDIT DEFENSES:
    • Adequate records maintained (burden of proof shifts to IRS)
    • Section 7491 — Burden of proof if you maintained records
    • Reasonable cause for errors
    • Reliance on professional advice
    • Statute of limitations expired
```

### Payment Plan Setup

```javascript
// IRS payment plan calculator

class IRSPaymentPlanCalculator {
  constructor(config) {
    this.totalOwed = config.totalOwed;
    this.interestRate = config.interestRate || 0.08; // ~8% annual
    this.penaltyRate = config.penaltyRate || 0.005; // ~0.5% monthly
    this.canPayInFull = config.canPayInFull || false;
    this.monthlyIncome = config.monthlyIncome || 0;
    this.monthlyExpenses = config.monthlyExpenses || 0;
    this.isLowIncome = config.isLowIncome || false;
  }

  getOptions() {
    const options = [];

    // Option 1: Pay in full
    options.push({
      name: 'Pay in Full',
      totalCost: this.totalOwed,
      monthlyPayment: this.totalOwed,
      monthsToPayoff: 1,
      setupFee: 0,
      totalInterest: 0,
      recommendation: 'Always best if possible',
      pros: [
        'No additional interest or penalties',
        'No setup fees',
        'Lien released quickly if applicable',
        'Case closed immediately',
      ],
      cons: [
        'Requires full payment now',
        'May deplete savings',
      ],
    });

    // Option 2: Short-term extension (120 days)
    const shortTermInterest = this._calculateInterest(120);
    options.push({
      name: 'Short-Term Extension (120 days)',
      totalCost: this.totalOwed + shortTermInterest,
      monthlyPayment: this.totalOwed / 4,
      monthsToPayoff: 4,
      setupFee: 0,
      totalInterest: shortTermInterest,
      recommendation: 'Good if you can pay within 120 days',
      pros: [
        'No setup fee',
        'No monthly payment requirement',
        'Can pay anytime within 120 days',
      ],
      cons: [
        'Interest continues to accrue',
        'Must pay in full by day 120',
      ],
    });

    // Option 3: Installment Agreement
    const monthlyThreshold = this.monthlyIncome - this.monthlyExpenses;
    const maxMonthly = Math.max(0, monthlyThreshold * 0.8);
    const setupFee = this.isLowIncome ? 43 : 31;
    const estimatedMonths = Math.ceil(this.totalOwed / Math.max(25, maxMonthly));
    const installmentInterest = this._calculateInstallmentInterest(estimatedMonths);

    options.push({
      name: 'Installment Agreement',
      totalCost: this.totalOwed + installmentInterest + setupFee,
      monthlyPayment: Math.max(25, maxMonthly),
      monthsToPayoff: estimatedMonths,
      setupFee: setupFee,
      totalInterest: installmentInterest,
      recommendation: 'Best for most taxpayers who cannot pay in full',
      pros: [
        'Affordable monthly payments',
        'Stops enforcement actions',
        'Can be modified if financial situation changes',
        'Relatively simple to apply online',
      ],
      cons: [
        'Setup fee required',
        'Interest continues to accrue',
        'Must stay current on all future filings',
        'Lien may still be filed for amounts over $25,000',
      ],
    });

    // Option 4: Offer in Compromise
    const offerAmount = Math.round(this._calculateOfferAmount() * 100) / 100;
    options.push({
      name: 'Offer in Compromise',
      totalCost: offerAmount + 205,
      monthlyPayment: offerAmount / (this.isLowIncome ? 12 : 5),
      monthsToPayoff: this.isLowIncome ? 12 : 5,
      setupFee: 205,
      totalInterest: 0,
      recommendation: 'Only if you truly cannot pay and have limited assets',
      pros: [
        'Settle for less than full amount',
        'Fresh start on tax compliance',
      ],
      cons: [
        '$205 application fee (non-refundable)',
        'Low approval rate (~40%)',
        'Complex application process',
        'Must stay compliant for 5 years',
        'May require professional help',
      ],
    });

    // Option 5: Currently Not Collectible
    options.push({
      name: 'Currently Not Collectible (CNC)',
      totalCost: this.totalOwed,
      monthlyPayment: 0,
      monthsToPayoff: null,
      setupFee: 0,
      totalInterest: null,
      recommendation: 'Last resort for severe financial hardship',
      pros: [
        'No payments required',
        'No collection actions while status active',
        'Can be reviewed annually',
      ],
      cons: [
        'Debt does not go away',
        'Interest and penalties continue to accrue',
        'Refunds may be applied to the debt',
        'Status must be renewed annually',
        'May have lien filed',
      ],
    });

    return options.sort((a, b) => a.totalCost - b.totalCost);
  }

  _calculateInterest(days) {
    return Math.round(
      this.totalOwed * (this.interestRate / 365) * days * 100
    ) / 100;
  }

  _calculateInstallmentInterest(months) {
    let remaining = this.totalOwed;
    let totalInterest = 0;
    for (let i = 0; i < months; i++) {
      const monthlyInterest = remaining * (this.interestRate / 12);
      totalInterest += monthlyInterest;
      const payment = Math.max(25, (this.monthlyIncome - this.monthlyExpenses) * 0.8);
      remaining -= (payment - monthlyInterest);
    }
    return Math.round(totalInterest * 100) / 100;
  }

  _calculateOfferAmount() {
    // Simplified OIC calculation
    const monthlyDisposable = Math.max(0,
      this.monthlyIncome - this.monthlyExpenses
    );
    const futureIncome = monthlyDisposable * 12;
    const assetEquity = 0; // Simplified
    return futureIncome + assetEquity;
  }

  getRecommendation() {
    const options = this.getOptions();
    const bestOption = options[0];

    if (this.canPayInFull) {
      return 'Pay in full — it is always the cheapest option.';
    }

    const disposableIncome = this.monthlyIncome - this.monthlyExpenses;

    if (disposableIncome <= 0) {
      return 'Consider Currently Not Collectible or an Offer in Compromise based on your financial situation.';
    }

    if (this.totalOwed / (disposableIncome * 0.8) <= 4) {
      return 'Consider a short-term extension (120 days) to pay in full without a setup fee.';
    }

    return 'An Installment Agreement is likely the best option for your situation.';
  }
```

## Conclusion

**Getting a letter from the IRS is not a disaster — but ignoring it is.** Most IRS notices are routine, correctable, and manageable with a clear response plan. The key principles:

- **Read the notice carefully** — identify the notice number, deadline, and required action
- **Respond on time** — the deadline is the most important factor in preventing escalation
- **Pay what you can** — partial payment is better than no payment
- **Request help when needed** — penalty abatement, payment plans, and professional representation are available
- **Keep records** — document every response and save all correspondence
- **Prevent future notices** — improve your filing accuracy and payment compliance

**The IRS wants compliance, not punishment. Most penalties can be reduced or removed, most balances can be paid over time, and most audits can be managed with proper documentation.**
